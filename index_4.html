Guitar Intervals & Triads

Interactive study guide · intervals summary based on the StringKick guide “Guitar Intervals: Explained Easily” (Just Rijna)

1. Why intervals matter

Intervals are the building blocks of music. They explain scales, chords, melodies and chord progressions – and they are also the key to navigating the fretboard. This page covers what intervals are, how to play them on guitar, how they build chords, and a four-step plan for learning them.

2. What is an interval?

An interval is simply the distance between two notes. It can be tiny (one fret) or large (the open low E to a note way up the neck). Saying “four frets apart” means little to non-guitarists, so each distance has a name. For example, fret 1 to fret 5 on the low E string is four frets – a major third.

There are twelve intervals. Each fret you climb adds one semitone:

Reading the names. m = minor, M = major, P = perfect, TT = tritone. Notice the patterns: a counting system (2nds, 3rds … 7ths), major/minor pairs, three “perfect” intervals (the 4th, the 5th and the octave), and one odd one out – the tritone. You don’t need to memorise this yet; just know there are twelve.

3. Playing intervals on guitar

Take the perfect fifth (7 frets). From the open low E, go up seven frets to fret 7. Start from fret 2 and it’s fret 9. The same logic works from any fret, and for every interval.

Across strings

Moving to the next string up is the same as sliding 5 frets along one string. So a perfect fifth on different strings is: (1) go up one string (= 5 frets), then (2) slide up 2 more frets (= 7 total). This is usually more comfortable than stretching along one string.

The G–B exception. Between the G and B strings the gap is only 4 frets, not 5. So when an interval crosses from G to B (or back), shift the pattern one fret. Try it in the explorer below – watch how the dots jump by one fret at the G/B boundary.

4. How to use intervals: building a chord

Every chord type has a recipe of intervals. The major chord recipe is:

Root + Major third (M3) + Perfect fifth (P5)

Build a C chord: the root is C (A string, fret 3). A major third above is E (4 frets up – D string fret 2). A perfect fifth above is G (7 frets up – open G string). Play those three together and you have C major. The full open C chord (x32010) just repeats the same ingredients in higher octaves – an extra root (C, B string fret 1) and an extra third (E, open high e). No new ingredients, only more of the same.

RootMajor 3rdPerfect 5th

Intervals also reveal why chords sound “happy” or “sad”, how melodies are put together, and how scales and progressions work. Page 2 uses this idea to unpick the open chords you already know.

5. How to learn intervals (4-step plan)

Don’t memorise all twelve in a list. Learn a few at a time and apply them to real music.

1. What is a triad?

A triad is a chord built from just three different notes, stacked from the intervals you met on Page 1:

TriadFormulaIntervals from rootFrets from rootSound
Major1 – 3 – 5M3 + P54 and 7bright, happy
Minor1 – ♭3 – 5m3 + P53 and 7dark, sad

The only difference between major and minor is the third: one fret lower makes it minor. The root and fifth stay put. (Stack a major third then a minor third = major; minor third then major third = minor.)

2. Why does a 6-string chord only have 3 notes?

An open chord such as E major uses six strings, but only three note names appear – some are simply doubled in different octaves. Every open major or minor chord is a triad with repeated notes (open 7th chords such as D7 or Am7 add a fourth note name). Learning to “see” the triad inside tells you which note is the root, which is the third (major or minor?), and what you can change.

3. Tutorial: deciphering an open chord

  1. Name each string’s note. Open strings are E A D G B E (low to high). Add one semitone per fret (E→F→F♯→G→G♯→A→A♯→B→C…).
  2. Find the root. It’s the note that matches the chord name. Mark every string playing it.
  3. Measure from the root. Count semitones up from the root to each other note. If the note is more than an octave above the root, subtract 12 (e.g. 16 becomes 4). Then: 0 = root, 3 = minor 3rd, 4 = major 3rd, 7 = perfect 5th.
  4. Label the dots R, 3 (or ♭3), 5. You should find only those three labels.
  5. Check major vs minor by looking at the third: 4 semitones above the root = major, 3 = minor.
Worked example – C major (x32010).
A string fret 3 = C → root (0)
D string fret 2 = E → 4 semitones above C → major 3rd
G string open = G → 7 semitones → perfect 5th
B string fret 1 = C → root again · high e open = E → 3rd again.
Result: C-E-G = R-3-5. A major triad. The low E string is muted – it would add an E (the 3rd) below the root, which changes the voicing.

5. Triads on string sets across the whole fretboard

The open chords above use four, five or six strings. Pull out just three adjacent strings and you get a compact triad you can play anywhere on the neck. Below, each chord is split into four string sets: strings 6–4 (E A D), strings 5–3 (A D G), strings 4–2 (D G B) and strings 3–1 (G B e). Every playable R-3-5 grip within a four-fret stretch is shown across frets 0–15.

Inversions. The lowest note of the grip decides the name. Root position has the root in the bass (R-3-5 going up), 1st inversion has the 3rd in the bass (3-5-R) and 2nd inversion has the 5th in the bass (5-R-3). Each set has all three, repeating every 12 frets. A ★ marks a grip that is already hiding inside the open (or barre) chord you know.
Watch the G–B shift. A grip that moves from strings 5–3 up to strings 3–1 crosses the G–B boundary, so the note on the B string moves one fret further down the neck than the others. Strings 4–2 and 3–1 both include that boundary. The finder works this out for you.

6. Patterns worth noticing

  • E and Em differ by one note only: G♯ (major 3rd, G string fret 1) becomes G (♭3, open G string). One finger lifted turns major into minor.
  • A, D, E are the same story from different roots – the root usually sits on the lowest played string, which is why you can name a chord by its bass note.
  • B and F use a barre to carry the shape up the neck: the same R-3-5 pattern, just with the nut replaced by your index finger.
  • Think of “moving a shape” as moving the root; the intervals around it never change.

Practice routine

  1. Play each chord slowly, string by string, saying the interval aloud (“root… fifth… root… third…”).
  2. Use quiz mode: hide the labels, name them from memory, then reveal.
  3. Pick a chord and find a compact three-string triad for it in section 5 above, then play it and compare the sound with the full open chord.
  4. Change the third by one fret (major ↔ minor) and listen to the mood change – try it on E/Em, A/Am and D/Dm. (On G the third is an open string and can’t be lowered, so skip that one.)
